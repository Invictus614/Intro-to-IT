    Section 01 • Foundations & Workforce

    IT Roles & Computing Disciplines

    Computing spans five academic disciplines and four operational role families. Administrators keep systems running securely, Architects blueprint scalable platforms, Engineers build and optimize them, and Analysts translate data and business problems into actionable solutions.

    Five Computing Disciplines

    Core Domains
    • Computer Engineering: Designs hardware systems and the low-level software that runs them.
    • Computer Science: Designs and implements software, solves computing problems, and innovates computing uses.
    • Software Engineering: Develops and maintains large-scale software systems systematically.
    • Information Systems: Integrates IT solutions to meet organizational goals, support communication, and process information.
    • Information Technology (IT): Focuses on managing, maintaining, and securing the technology infrastructure that supports an organization.

    Four Role Families Overview

    Career Architecture
    • Administrators: Manage and maintain systems, networks, and databases; handle troubleshooting, settings, and security protocols.
    • Architects: Design and plan IT systems, networks, or apps with a focus on scalability, security, and business needs.
    • Engineers: Use technical expertise, coding, and hardware configuration to design, build, optimize, and adapt systems.
    • Analysts: Investigate data to uncover patterns, trends, and inefficiencies and propose solutions to complex challenges.

    Section 02 • Architecture & Evolution

    Computer Hardware & Five Generations

    Computing evolved from mechanical calculators and vacuum tubes to silicon microprocessors and emerging parallel AI systems. Inside every modern system, the Von Neumann IPOS cycle (Input, Process, Output, Storage) connects the CPU, registers, buses, RAM, and non-volatile storage.

    Pre-Electronic Computing Milestones

    Historical Foundations
    The Abacus Beads strung on rods in a frame store values by position; requires a human operator to calculate, otherwise it is merely data storage.
    Leibniz Machine Gears set mechanically to represent inputs and final state outputs; algorithm for basic arithmetic embedded directly in hardware.
    Babbage & Ada Lovelace Babbage's Analytical Engine (unbuilt) was programmable via punched paper cards; Ada Lovelace proved it programmable and is the world's first programmer.
    ENIAC First general-purpose electronic computer and one of the earliest programmable electronic computers (1st Generation).
    Generation Core Hardware Memory & Storage I/O, OS & Languages Characteristics & Examples
    1st (1946–1959) Vacuum Tubes Magnetic drums (main); punch cards (external). Punch cards in, printouts out. Machine language. No OS (human operators set switches). Enormous, hot, unreliable, power-hungry. ENIAC, UNIVAC, IBM-701, IBM-650.
    2nd (1959–1965) Transistors (binary 0/1 states) Magnetic core memory; tapes and discs. Punch cards & printouts. Assembly language; early FORTRAN & COBOL. Humans handled punch cards. Smaller, cheaper, faster, less energy, used in atomic energy; still needed A/C. Honeywell 400, IBM 7094, CDC 1604/3600, UNIVAC 1108.
    3rd (1965–1971) Integrated Circuits (ICs) on silicon semiconductors Improved magnetic disks (Floppy Disk introduced). Keyboards (input), monitors (output). High-level languages. Operating systems enabled multitasking & resource sharing. Cheaper, faster, smaller, more reliable, though complex to manufacture. PDP-8, PDP-11, ICL 2900, IBM 360, IBM 370.
    4th (1971–Present) Microprocessors (thousands of ICs on one chip) RAM/ROM, hard drives, SSDs, optical/flash storage. GUIs, mouse, handheld input. High-level languages & app software. MS-DOS, Microsoft Windows, macOS. Massive size reduction, portable, economical, universal. IBM first home computer, Apple Macintosh, modern PCs.
    5th (Emerging) ULSI (Ultra Large Scale Integration) & parallel processing High-density distributed & molecular/quantum media. Natural language input and AI software ecosystems. Supports Artificial Intelligence, Natural Language Processing, Quantum Computing, Molecular Tech, Nanotechnology.

    CPU, Control Unit & 5 Registers

    Clock: GHz
    • ALU (Arithmetic Logic Unit): Executes math (add/subtract) and logical comparisons.
    • Control Unit: Transfers data from RAM into registers, informs ALU which registers hold operands, activates ALU, and routes results.
    • MAR (Memory Address Register): Holds the memory location of data/instruction to access.
    • MDR (Memory Data Register): Holds actual data moving between RAM and CPU.
    • AC (Accumulator): Stores results of ALU arithmetic/logic operations.
    • PC (Program Counter): Stores the address of the next instruction to execute.
    • CIR (Current Instruction Register): Holds the instruction currently being decoded and executed.

    System Unit, Buses & Memory

    IPOS & Von Neumann
    • 3 Buses: Address Bus (carries destination addresses), Data Bus (transfers data between CPU/RAM/I/O), Control Bus (carries timing & command signals).
    • Motherboard & Clock: Main circuit board (soldered + socketed parts, expansion slots/cards, USB/Serial/Parallel ports, power supply). System clock sends regular electrical pulses (1 instruction per pulse).
    • GPU: Processes 3D graphics and parallel computational workloads beyond rendering.
    • RAM (Primary Memory): Fast, directly accessible by CPU, volatile/ephemeral (lost on power-off). Used for active multitasking.
    • Secondary Storage: Non-volatile (HDD spinning magnetic disks; SSD faster flash chips; flash drives, tape, CD/DVD). Measured in MB, GB, TB.
    • Bit & Byte: A bit is 0 or 1; 8 bits = 1 byte (enough to store one character such as "M").
    System Class Architecture & Portability Primary Use Cases
    SupercomputersLargest/fastest; thousands of interconnected processors.Weather forecasting, scientific simulations, nuclear energy research.
    MainframesSupport hundreds/thousands of simultaneous users & multi-program workloads.Large enterprises processing high-volume transactions & records.
    WorkstationsSingle-user technical systems; high-speed CPU, large RAM, pro GPUs.Graphics design, music production, CAD/engineering design.
    DesktopsFixed, heavy, separate units; best consumer performance & expandability.Office, gaming, heavy personal productivity.
    LaptopsIntegrated portable unit under ~7 lbs; balances speed and mobility.Mobile work, study, travel computing.
    Handheld / MobilePocket-sized under 1 lb; integrated screen, limited graphics.Communication, quick data access, personal management.

    Section 03 • Connectivity, Cloud & Web

    Networking, Cloud Models & The Internet

    Networks scale from personal Bluetooth PANs to global WANs across physical media and routing hardware. Above the TCP/IP and DNS layers sit cloud service abstractions (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS) and the World Wide Web powered by URLs, HTTP, HTML, and XML.

    Network Types & Topologies

    PAN / LAN / WAN
    • PAN / LAN / WAN: PAN (short-range, e.g. wireless earbuds), LAN (single building/complex), WAN (long-distance/global).
    • Open vs. Closed Networks: Open (public domain like Internet TCP/IP, no license fee); Closed/Proprietary (company-controlled for fees, e.g. Apple App Store).
    • Bus Topology: Single central coaxial backbone; cheap/simple, but a backbone break fails the entire network.
    • Star Topology: All nodes connect to a central hub/switch; easy to troubleshoot, single point of failure at the hub.
    • Ring Topology: Closed loop where each node links to two neighbors; no central hub, but one break downs the ring.
    • Mesh & Hybrid: Mesh interconnects every node (maximum redundancy/fault-tolerance, expensive); Hybrid combines topologies.

    Hardware, Media & Models

    OSI Physical → Network
    • Communication Models: Client/Server (clients request, central server fulfills, e.g. print server); Peer-to-Peer (P2P) (devices both request & serve, e.g. Gnutella, BitTorrent, gaming).
    • Distributed Systems: Cluster (independent machines acting as one for HA/load balancing); Grid (loosely coupled workload distribution); Cloud (scalable rented virtual resources like EC2).
    • Transmission Media: UTP (cheap Ethernet, max 100m, EMI-sensitive); STP (metallic foil blocks EMI); Coaxial (copper + braided shield); Fiber-Optic (light pulses, highest speed); Wireless (Wi-Fi/Bluetooth).
    • Connecting Devices: Repeater (amplifies signal, e.g. 300ft→500ft); Bridge (links network segments & filters MACs); Switch (sends only to intended recipient, cutting traffic up to 99%); Router (gateway using IP routing tables between LANs/WANs).

    Internet Addressing & DNS

    IPv4 / IPv6 / ICANN
    • IPv4 vs. IPv6: IPv4 uses 32-bit dotted-decimal addresses (exhausted unique supply); IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses alongside legacy IPv4.
    • DNS & Resolution: Hierarchical, decentralized system translating mnemonic domain names (e.g. wgu.edu) into numeric IP addresses via root servers and TLD servers.
    • Top-Level Domains (TLDs): .com (Commercial), .gov (Government), .org (Nonprofit), .tv (TV stations), plus country-codes (.uk, .de).
    • ICANN & DARPA: ICANN (nonprofit) coordinates operations and awards IP blocks to ISPs; DARPA pioneered early internet research.

    World Wide Web: HTML vs. XML

    Hypertext & Markup
    • WWW Components: Hypertext document format with links, HTTP transfer protocol, web server supplying pages, browser rendering text/image/audio/video.
    • HTML (HyperText Markup Language): Focuses on appearance and presentation on screen using predefined tags for layout, links, and multimedia.
    • XML (Extensible Markup Language): Focuses on semantics — describes, stores, and transports structured data as standardized text across different systems.

    Section 04 • System Software, Licensing & Code

    Operating Systems, Licensing & Programming

    The Operating System kernel bridges hardware and applications, while software licensing models dictate whether code can be inspected, shared, or modified. Programs express step-by-step algorithms translated to machine code by compilers or interpreters.

    Operating Systems & Utilities

    Kernel & File Systems
    • OS & Kernel: Manages hardware, memory, file systems, and user accounts; the Kernel is the core bridge between hardware and applications.
    • Windows: Closed-source GUI OS (Microsoft); directory paths use backslashes (\), root typically C:\.
    • macOS: Closed-source, UNIX-based OS (Apple); noted for creative workflows.
    • Linux: Open-source OS allowing kernel modification; dominates servers; paths use forward slashes (/) from root /.
    • Mobile OS: Google Android (open-source) vs. Apple iOS (closed-source).
    • Utilities & Drivers: Utility software optimizes/protects systems (antivirus, disk cleanup); Device drivers enable OS-to-hardware communication.

    Algorithms, Compilers & Interpreters

    Execution Models
    • Algorithm: Well-organized, step-by-step instructions or mathematical method to solve a problem (like a recipe); at the heart of machine learning.
    • Compiler: Checks all code for errors and translates the entire program into machine language before execution (e.g. C++, Java). Faster execution; must recompile after edits.
    • Interpreter: Translates and executes scripts line-by-line (e.g. Python, Perl). Enables real-time testing, but halts immediately upon hitting an error.

    Open-SourceSource code & kernel open for global collaboration and modification.Linux, Android (AOSP).
    Bespoke vs. Off-the-ShelfBespoke: custom-built, high control, expensive. Off-the-shelf: mass-produced, cheaper, vendor-controlled.Custom ERP vs. retail productivity suite.

    Section 05 • DIKW, Data Quality & Relational Queries

    Databases, Data Pyramid & SQL Operations

    Organizations transform raw facts into strategic decisions along the Data Pyramid (DIKW), enforce data hygiene and five quality attributes, and store records in structured relational or flexible NoSQL/cloud databases queried with SQL.

    DIKW Tier Key Words Health Care Example Smartwatch & Amazon Examples
    Data Raw, unprocessed facts, numbers, unstructured observations. Lab test readings, raw patient records, device logs. Steps, heart rate, calories; browsing clicks & search queries.
    Information Organized, structured, processed, summarized, categorized. Formatted charts so doctors/nurses interpret results. Graphs/notifications; popular product & inventory tracking.
    Knowledge Patterns, insights, relationships, causation, trends. Correlating symptoms, test results, and medical histories. Linking sleep quality to activity/HR; forecasting demand trends.
    Wisdom Decide, strategic, recommend, optimize, predict, implement. Making informed clinical decisions on patient treatment. Personalized health coaching; tailored offers & faster logistics.

    Data Types, Hygiene & Quality

    3 Steps: Capture → Analyze → Use
    • 5 Data Types: Integers (whole: 4, 34), Floating Point (decimals: 9.5, 1.7), Characters (single: F, !, g), Strings (sequence: Abc, 3erf78!@), Boolean (True/False).
    • Structured vs. Unstructured & Big Data: Structured (coded tables); Unstructured (video, comments); Big Data (massive, fast-growing datasets exceeding traditional tools).
    • 5 Quality Attributes: Precise (context accuracy), Valid (meets collection rules), Reliable (consistent anywhere), Timely (collected on time), Complete (full picture).
    • 5 Dirty Data Problems (Hygiene): Duplicate (identical records), Conflicting (same record, different attributes), Incomplete (missing fields), Invalid (non-standard), Unsynchronized (not shared across systems).

    DBMS & 6 Database Models

    PK / FK Architecture
    • DBMS & Keys: Application layer requests data via the DBMS layer (never directly). Records contain fields; Primary Key (PK) uniquely identifies a row; Foreign Key (FK) links to another table's PK.
    • Flat-File: Single plain-text delimited file (e.g. CSV); one record per line; no multi-table joins.
    • Hierarchical: Oldest model; predictable parent/child tree structure.
    • Relational: Tables logically joined via Primary and Foreign Keys.
    • Object-Oriented / NoSQL / Cloud: OODB stores native objects; NoSQL uses flexible document/key-value/graph formats at scale; Cloud DBs run remotely as managed services; Data Lakes hold vast raw unstructured data.

    Section 06 • Software Engineering Lifecycle

    SDLC Phases, Methodologies & Testing

    The System Development Life Cycle moves from defining what a system must do to designing how it works, coding, validating quality through white-box and three-stage black-box testing, and sustaining operations across four maintenance modes.

    5 SDLC Phases & Maintenance

    Core Lifecycle
    • 1. Requirements Analysis: Defines what the system must do to solve user/business needs.
    • 2. Design: Defines how it will be built (architecture, UI, database schemas).
    • 3. Implementation: Developers translate designs into executable code and build databases.
    • 4. Testing: Verifies code logic, usability, and stakeholder alignment before release.
    • 5. Maintenance (4 Types): Corrective (fix bugs), Adaptive (update for OS/hardware changes), Perfective (enhance speed/features), Preventative (proactive updates to avoid future failure).

    Development Models

    Sequential vs. Iterative
    • Waterfall: Linear, sequential phases; each phase must complete before the next begins.
    • Rapid Prototyping: Builds early visual mock-ups to validate requirements quickly via user feedback.
    • Agile: Iterative, flexible approach emphasizing continuous feedback and rapid delivery of small functional increments.
    • Scrum: Agile framework using fixed-length iterations (sprints), prioritized backlogs, and daily check-ins.
    • Extreme Programming (XP): Agile practice emphasizing technical excellence via pair programming and test-driven development.
    Testing Concept / Stage Perspective & Purpose Practical Scope
    Pareto Principle (80/20)80% of software errors originate in 20% of the system.Focuses test sampling on high-risk modules.
    Glass-Box (White-Box)Developers inspect and test internal code structure directly.Basis Path Testing: ensures every instruction/independent path runs at least once.
    Black-Box TestingTester has no knowledge of internal code; focuses on UX and behavior.Progresses sequentially across Alpha → Beta → User Acceptance.
    Stage 1: AlphaInternal developers or internal UX team test preliminary build.Finds bugs, usability flaws, and performance issues before external release.
    Stage 2: Beta (Pilot)Limited segment of real end-users test in real-world scenarios.Captures external UX feedback prior to general release.
    Stage 3: User Acceptance (UAT)Users test complete system in an operational setting.Confirms alignment with business objectives and stakeholder sign-off.

    Section 07 • Delivery Governance

    Project Management Lifecycle, Goals & Risks

    Every IT project progresses through four phases—Initiation, Planning, Execution, and Closure—anchored by SMART or CLEAR goals, five formal planning artifacts, and proactive risk mitigation across budget, staffing, technology, and integration.

    Phase Core Activities & Deliverables
    1. Project InitiationBegins with a business case followed by a feasibility study to justify value and viability.
    2. Project PlanningDefines scope, drafts roadmap & project management plan (resources, cost, time estimates), sets SMART/CLEAR goals, and completes 5 core planning documents.
    3. Project ExecutionStarts with a kickoff meeting; develops project team, assigns resources, sets up KPI tracking, conducts status meetings, monitors timetable, and builds deliverables.
    4. Project ClosureDeclares project complete, holds retrospective meetings (strengths & improvements), completes final documentation & financial reports, and dissolves team.

    SMART vs. CLEAR Goals

    Goal Frameworks
    • SMART: Specific (who, what, where, when, which, why), Measurable (success criteria), Attainable (feasible with resources), Realistic (willingness to work toward it), Timely (achievable in timeframe).
    • CLEAR: Collaborative (teamwork), Limited (bounded scope/time), Emotional (taps into employee passion), Appreciable (large goals broken into quick wins), Refinable (flexible as situations evolve).

    5 Planning Documents

    End of Planning
    • Scope Statement: Outlines objectives, deliverables, and milestones for the team.
    • Work Breakdown Structure (WBS): Breaks the project into manageable work segments.
    • Milestones: High-level checkpoints/goals to meet throughout project duration.
    • Communication Plan: Frequency and methods for updating stakeholders.
    • Risk Management Plan: Identifies foreseeable risks including cost overruns and delays.
    Risk Categories & Mitigation Execution Risks (budget, people, technology, requirements — e.g. cost overrun, insufficient staff, inadequate tools, weak stakeholder support) are best mitigated by planning in advance. Integration Risks (disruptions to existing systems) are mitigated by assessing disruptions, securing stakeholder support, and shared understanding of complexity. Risks of the Unknown are caught early via frequent monitoring of KPIs/milestones and open participant communication.

    Section 08 • Governance, Defense & Resilience

    Security, Ethics, Laws & Business Continuity

    Protecting organizational systems unites ethical culture, the CIA Triad, U.S. regulatory compliance, proactive technical defenses against malware and network attacks, and resilient disaster recovery strategies scaled by recovery speed and cost.

    CIA Triad & Ethics

    Core Security Pillars
    • Confidentiality: Rules limiting data access based on potential harm; enforced primarily via access controls & encryption.
    • Integrity: Keeps data trustworthy, accurate, and unaltered in transit or at rest via permissions, version control, and redundant copies.
    • Availability: Reliable access for authorized users via hardware maintenance, compatibility testing, and timely OS/app patching.
    • Ethics, Privacy & Security: Ethics (moral standards shaped by organizational expectations, experiences, philosophy, values, and Code of Conduct); Privacy (proper governance of personal data); Security (preventing unauthorized theft/exploitation).

    Malware, Cyber Attacks & Defenses

    Threats & Controls
    • Malware (Intentional Harm): Viruses (attach to host programs), Worms (self-replicate autonomously across networks), Spyware (covertly logs credentials/cards), Phishing (deceptive impersonation emails).
    • Network & Password Attacks: DoS (floods resources to break availability), Packet Sniffing / MITM (intercepts traffic), Brute Force (tries all combinations), Dictionary (common word lists), Rainbow Table (matches stored password hashes back to plaintext).
    • 5 Key Defenses: Firewalls (filter trusted/untrusted traffic), Proxy Servers (intermediary shielding clients), Network Auditing (detects traffic anomalies), Antivirus (removes known malware), MFA & Encryption (hardens access & protects data in transit).

    Business Continuity & Disaster Recovery

    Five Nines: 99.999%
    • Uptime & High Availability: "Five nines" (99.999%) permits only ~3 seconds of downtime/week; High Availability maintains live system replicas for immediate failover.
    • DRP vs. BCP: Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP) restores IT systems/data after catastrophic failure; Business Continuity Plan (BCP) resumes vital business operations with limited resources while auxiliary services wait.
    • Storage Strategies: Data Mirroring (simultaneous multi-drive writes), Tape Archival (cheap, portable), Off-site Storage (salt mines/caves/cloud multi-region). Assess off-site sites by Accessibility, Cost, Environment, Geographic area, and Security.
    • Hot vs. Cold Storage: Hot (Azure Hot Blobs, AWS Hot Storage — fastest recovery, premium hardware, high cost); Cold (Tape, Amazon Glacier, Google Coldline — slower recovery, minimal cost).

    IT Law Bodies & Agencies

    Standards & Oversight
    • CPSR: Global organization promoting responsible technology use via education.
    • FCC: Independent U.S. agency regulating radio, TV, wire, satellite, and cable communications.
    • ITS (NTIA): Research/engineering lab advancing U.S. telecom and information infrastructure.
    • NIST: Develops standards, measurements, and frameworks for reliable, interoperable, secure systems.
    • NSA: U.S. agency protecting vital defense, military, and national infrastructure networks (e.g. power grid).
